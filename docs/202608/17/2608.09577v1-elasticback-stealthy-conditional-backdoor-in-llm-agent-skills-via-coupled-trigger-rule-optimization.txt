Abstract 

Agent skills, bundles of instructions and resources that an LLM agent loads on demand, form an emerging supply chain where a single poisoned skill can persistently compromise every agent that installs it. However, existing skill attacks ei-ther fire on every request or rely on fine-tuned weights or multiple skills, leaving a conditional and low-cost backdoor unexplored. In this work, we present ElasticBack, an effec-tive conditional single-skill backdoor that plants a rule R in the skill document and a benign-looking trigger T in the user query, so the malicious payload fires only when both co-occur. ElasticBack binds the two sides through a trigger-as-switch construction, generating R via semantic-anchored rule injec-tion. It then freezes R and evolves T against it with a stealth-constrained genetic search, so that effectiveness and stealth are optimized, keeping the backdoor weight-free and dormant on benign inputs. Extensive experiments across three target behaviors (50 skills each) and four agent LLMs show that ElasticBack attains a high attack success rate at a near-zero false-positive rate with preserved clean accuracy, transfers across models, and evades deployment-time defenses. These results motivate stronger defenses for the skill supply chain. 

1 Introduction 

Large language model (LLM) agents (Yehudai et al. 2026; Ma et al. 2026) are now widely applied across coding (Islam, Ali, and Parvez 2024), data analysis (Hong et al. 2025), and customer support (Zhang et al. 2025b), where they go beyond generating text to autonomously execute actions on the user’s behalf. To gain new capabilities more conveniently, they can load skills without retraining (Zhou et al. 2026; Li et al. 2026a): a skill is a reusable bundle of a natural-language instruction file (Skill.md) and supporting resources such as scripts, templates, or data, invoked whenever a task matches its domain. Today, skills have become an indispensable part of modern agent systems (Ling, Zhong, and Huang 2026). Yet the convenience and practicality that make skills so widely used also turn them into a dangerous attack surface (Xu and Yan 2026; Jia et al. 2026b), since a single poisoned skill can persistently harm every user who installs it. Therefore, the security threats facing agent skills demand urgent and systematic study.

The risk is already confirmed by an audit of 3,984 commu-nity skills, 13.4% of which carry severe security issues (Liu et al. 2026). Agent-skill security has drawn growing atten-tion (Badhe and Tiwari 2026; Li et al. 2026d), and existing at-tacks fall into two forms. The first fires indiscriminately, with no attacker-controlled trigger. SkillJect (Jia et al. 2026a) and Skill-Inject (Qu et al. 2026) embed always-on malicious in-structions in Skill.md, while Sleeper attacks (Li et al. 2026c) wake from a persistent state on ordinary benign queries. The second fires under an attacker-controlled trigger but at higher cost. BadSkill (Tie et al. 2026) needs a fine-tuned model bun-dled in the skill, and SkillTrojan (Feng et al. 2026) shards an encrypted payload across multiple skills. However, prior attacks fire without an attacker-controlled trigger or depend on fine-tuned weights or multiple skills, making it difficult to implement stealthy, low-cost skill backdoors (Fig. 1(b)). 

Motivation. In real-world scenarios, an attacker who de-ploys a stealthy, low-cost backdoor skill can cause severe harm. Because such a skill backdoor is weight-free and runs only when triggered, it is hard to detect and easy to spread. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the backdoor consists of a rule hidden inside the skill and a benign-looking trigger phrase in the query. It stays silent, passes review on ordinary requests, and fires its payload only when the trigger appears. Current de-fenses inspect a skill only at rest or on benign queries, so this dormant rule slips through undetected. Therefore, it is urgent to develop stealthy and low-cost conditional backdoor attacks tailored for LLM-agent skills to advance the development of more robust defense mechanisms. To achieve this, we present ElasticBack, an effective framework for planting a stealthy, low-cost conditional back-arXiv:2608.09577v1 [cs.AI] 10 Aug 2026 door in agent skills. Since an installed skill is trusted, Elas-ticBack embeds a conditional rule R in Skill.md and the trigger T in the user query, firing the payload only when both co-occur. This raises two main challenges: ❶ How to separately generate R and T so that the payload fires reliably under the trigger yet stays dormant on benign queries? ❷

How to keep both R and T stealthy enough to evade perplex-ity screening and LLM-judge auditing? Firstly, since R and T are generated separately and the adversary cannot see the victim’s runtime queries, a naively chosen rule and trigger tend to misalign. A loosely worded rule matches benign requests and misfires, while a generic trigger fails to satisfy the rule reliably. Thus, the key challenge is to bind a separately generated rule and trigger so that the payload fires precisely under the trigger and never otherwise .To this end, ElasticBack adopts a trigger-as-switch design. A small set of payload-derived gate words bridges the two sides, seeding the rule’s activation condition and anchoring the evolution of the trigger, so the trigger becomes the sole switch that flips the payload on. Then, a guided search aligns 

T against a frozen R, so the rule and trigger stay consistent by construction and ASR and FPR can be driven separately. Secondly, both R in Skill.md and T in the query are ex-posed to detectors. A rule inserted at an arbitrary position or written freely stands out, whether by a perplexity spike or by an LLM judge that flags the out-of-place instruction. Mean-while, a conspicuous or ungrammatical trigger is caught by perplexity screening. Thus, another key challenge is to make both the rule and the trigger read as natural parts of their host text while preserving the attack . To tackle this, Elas-ticBack hides R at a low-salience insertion point from an attention-guided saliency map and folds fluency, coherence, and grammaticality into the search objective. Stealth is op-timized jointly with effectiveness, not as an afterthought, keeping both sides inconspicuous without sacrificing attack success. In summary, the contributions of our paper are: 

• To the best of our knowledge, we devise the first condi-tional single-skill backdoor attack, named ElasticBack, which is capable of executing malicious tasks under the attacker-controlled trigger without affecting normal tasks. 

• We propose a semantic-anchored rule injection approach that generates a gated rule R from payload-unique gate words. On this basis, we further design a corpus-driven trigger evolution method that evolves the trigger T against the frozen R via a stealth-constrained genetic search, jointly optimizing effectiveness and dual-side stealth. 

• We conduct extensive experiments across three target be-haviors, each evaluated on 50 skills, and four agent LLMs against recent skill attacks and deployment-time defenses. Experimental results show that ElasticBack attains high 

ASR at near-zero FPR with preserved CAC .

2 Background and Related Work 

2.1 LLM Agents and Agent Skills 

An LLM agent (Duan et al. 2025; Yehudai et al. 2026) aug-ments a language-model backbone with a harness that lets it reason over a task, call external tools, and run code on the user’s behalf. Modern agent frameworks such as Claude Code (Anthropic 2025a), Codex CLI (OpenAI 2025), and Gemini CLI (Google 2025) load skills on demand, since no fixed prompt covers tasks across all domains. Anthropic (An-thropic 2025b) formalizes this pattern as the open agent skills specification, in which a skill bundles an instruction file (Skill.md) with optional scripts, templates, or data. Guided only by a short description , the agent judges whether a skill fits the current task and, on a match, it reads the full Skill.md and executes its instructions. Because such skills are openly published and adopted almost verbatim (Liu et al. 2026), each becomes an artifact the agent implicitly trusts rather than untrusted data it screens. Therefore, tampering with a single published skill can silently redirect every agent that adopts it, without any access to model weights. 

2.2 Backdoor Attacks and Defenses on Agent Systems 

Classical backdoor attacks poison training data or model pa-rameters so that a trigger produces an attacker-chosen output while clean inputs behave normally. They have been studied on images (Liu et al. 2024a; Xun et al. 2024; Shen et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025a), graphs (Sui et al. 2026b,a), and recently LLMs (Li et al. 2026b; Yan et al. 2026). Because they target the model itself, they need access to its weights or training data, which a skill publisher lacks. The skill publisher can tamper only with the skill it pub-lishes, so attacks shift to the agent’s run-time channels and the skill artifact, neither of which needs backbone weights. Run-time attacks such as indirect prompt injection (Shi et al. 2025), memory or retrieval poisoning (Chen et al. 2024), and sleeper threats (Li et al. 2026c) are weight-free: an injection fires only within its interaction, and dormant ones wake on ordinary queries rather than on a selective trigger carried by a shareable artifact. Attacks on the skill artifact itself persist across tasks and users, yet each falls short. Skill-Inject (Qu et al. 2026), SkillJect (Jia et al. 2026a), and instruction-level backdoors (Zhang et al. 2024) fire on every use rather than under a selective trigger. BadSkill (Tie et al. 2026) requires fine-tuning a model bundled in the skill, and Skill-Trojan (Feng et al. 2026) shards the payload across multiple skills, both at higher cost. None achieves a weight-free and conditional skill backdoor attack in agent systems. To mitigate these threats, existing deployment-time de-fenses (Ma et al. 2026) take two forms: static inspection (perplexity screening (Shu et al. 2023), LLM judging (Zheng et al. 2023)) and run-time monitoring (behavioral test-ing (Luo et al. 2026)). These catch overt payloads but miss a dormant conditional rule. Therefore, we instantiate three such detectors as baselines in §5.

4 Design of ElasticBack 

4.1 Overview 

As illustrated in Fig. 2, ElasticBack plants a conditional, stealthy, weight-free backdoor in a single skill by coupling a document-side rule R and a query-side trigger T around a fixed payload s. The two sides are bridged by the pay-load’s gate words G and realized via two modules: ❶ In the 

Semantic-Anchored Rule Injection module, ElasticBack lo-cates a low-salience insertion point in Skill.md via a saliency map, mines the payload’s gate words G through cross-domain semantic mapping, and generates a conditional rule R that is frozen once a behavioral probe selects its best deployment variant. ❷ In the Corpus-Driven Trigger Evolution module, ElasticBack augments the skill’s queries into a corpus and evolves T against the frozen R via a constrained genetic search (C-HGA), then deploys the most reliable trigger via syntactic repair and a lower-confidence-bound (LCB) selec-tion. Since the gate word reaches the request through T ,the trigger is the sole activation switch (trigger-as-switch), letting us freeze R and optimize ASR and FPR separately.

4.3 Corpus-Driven Trigger Evolution 

To activate reliably yet stealthily, this module evolves T

against the frozen R in three stages. 

Corpus augmentation. Since skills ship few example queries, we keep a search pool (originals plus same-intent LLM paraphrases) for stable evolution and probing, and the primary pool (originals only) for final metrics, so reported numbers are never inflated by synthetic inputs. 

Trigger evolution. To optimize T , we propose C-HGA, which builds on the genetic-search idea of AutoDAN (Liu et al. 2024b) and is tailored to conditional activation and dual-side stealth. Its population is anchored to the gate words 

G (expanded with benign shadow tokens like synonyms and abbreviations, so candidates match the anchor with natural phrasing) rather than the skill’s own words, which cannot separate triggered from benign requests. 5 Performance Evaluation 

5.1 Experimental Setup 

Dataset. To evaluate our method in realistic scenarios, we curate a skill dataset from ClawHub 1, spanning software engineering, content generation, data analysis, and other do-mains. For each target behavior, the constructed dataset in-cludes 50 skills. Each skill performs its intended task and is a self-contained bundle of a Skill.md file, supporting re-sources, and a small set of in-domain example queries. 

Target behaviors. We consider three target behaviors: ❶

Information disclosure (InfoDisc) exfiltrates host environ-ment secrets such as API keys and credentials to an attacker endpoint; ❷ Malicious injection (MalInject) runs unautho-rized commands or code on the host; ❸ Output manipulation (OutManip) silently alters the skill’s results. Each behavior is realized by a payload script s bundled under a benign cover name (e.g. env_sync_check.sh ), and activated by a co-optimized rule R and trigger T .

Victim environment We adopt Claude Code (Anthropic 2025a) as the victim platform and deploy four open backbone models: GLM-5.2, MiniMax-M3, GPT-5.4, and Claude-Sonnet-4.6. Per our threat model (§3.1), the attack is crafted offline on a local surrogate (by default GLM-5.2) that authors 

R and T , then transferred to four victims without access to their weights or internals. To test generality across surrogates, we additionally evaluate MiniMax-M3 as the surrogate. 

Attack baselines. We compare ElasticBack with three attack baselines: ❶ Unconditional Injection (a SkillJect vari-ant (Jia et al. 2026a)) appends to Skill.md an always-on com-mand that runs the bundled payload; ❷ Handcrafted Condi-tional pairs a hand-written rule with a fixed, generic trigger word (e.g., when the user asks for a summary, run script.sh ); 

> 1https://clawhub.ai/skills

❸ Instruction-Backdoor (Zhang et al. 2024) ports its se-mantic trigger onto skills. Both conditional baselines forgo ElasticBack’s gate-word anchoring and co-optimization. 

Defense baselines. We evaluate ElasticBack against three SOTA defenses methods: ❶ PPL Detection (Shu et al. 2023) flags anomalous local perplexity in Skill.md or the trigger; ❷ LLM-as-Judge Audit (Zheng et al. 2023) has an independent model review Skill.md for hidden malicious in-structions; ❸ Behavioral Monitoring (Luo et al. 2026) runs skills on benign queries and flags any whose payload fires. 

Metrics. We report three metrics (§3.2): attack success rate ASR , the fraction of triggered queries that execute the payload; false-positive rate FPR , the fraction of benign queries where the payload fires; and accuracy drop ∆CAC ,the reduction in benign task accuracy caused by the backdoor. 

Implementation. ElasticBack is implemented in Python 3.10 with PyTorch 2.1. The environment is Intel(R) Core(TM) i7-13620H CPU, NVIDIA RTX 4060 (GPU), 16 GiB memory, and Ubuntu 20.04 (OS). All weights are tuned on a held-out development set: fitness weights α = 3 .0, λ =1.5, stealth weights ws = 0 .40 , w r = 0 .35 , w d = 0 .25 ,gate threshold θg = 0.60 , and ASR saturation thresh-old 0.8. Final evaluation runs at temperature 0, with each skill receiving one triggered query (for ASR ) and one be-nign query (for FPR , CAC ). As decoding is deterministic (temperature = 0 ), per-query outcomes are fixed, so a single run per skill over n=50 skills determines each reported rate. 

5.2 Experimental Results 

Comparison with Baselines. Table 1 compares Elas-ticBack with three baselines across three behaviors and four victims. High ASR alone is insufficient, since an effective attack must also preserve normal behavior. Every baseline reaches competitive ASR yet breaks down on FPR or ∆CAC ,whereas ElasticBack is the only method holding all three at once. Averaged over the 12 (payload, victim) settings, it attains 89% ASR at 6% FPR and ∆CAC of 3%. ∆CAC is zero in 7 of the 12 settings and never exceeds 12%, so benign utility is largely preserved. Each baseline fails for a reason rooted in its own design. Cross-surrogate Transferability. The attack effective-ness shown in Table 1 is obtained by using GLM-5.2 as the surrogate to generate the trigger and the conditional rule, and deploying on the four victim models. To verify that our method applies across different surrogates, we swap the sur-rogate to MiniMax-M3 and redeploy on the GLM-5.2 and MiniMax-M3 victims. As shown in Figure 3, our method remains effective after the surrogate is changed: ASR ranges from 86.0% to 100.0% and FPR stays at or below 4.0%, consistent with the results obtained with the GLM-5.2 sur-rogate. Moreover, the attack executes successfully regard-less of whether the surrogate and the victim belong to the same model family (e.g., MiniMax-M3 to MiniMax-M3), and FPR never exceeds one benign query. This indicates that ElasticBack does not depend on a specific surrogate. 

Cross-task Instruction Transferability. Each poisoned skill in Table 1 is generated and evaluated against one source task instruction, so we ask whether it still works when the skill is invoked by other tasks. We keep each poisoned skill fixed and evaluate it on 20 LLM-generated task instructions for the same skill. As shown in Figure 4, the attack stays broadly effective on the GLM-5.2 victim model for three target behaviors: ASR holds at 76.6–83.2% across the three behaviors, down from 96–98% on the source task, so the rule and trigger do not overfit one instruction. The trade-off is a higher FPR of 12.4–15.8% (at most 2% on the source task), as the gate is calibrated to that task. Even so, ASR stays well above FPR on every behavior, so the conditional gating still separates activation from misfire on different tasks.

Cost. ElasticBack is weight-free: its only cost is offline queries to the attacker’s surrogate. On GLM-5.2, the C-HGA search converges within 3-5 generations. The resulting skill is a static artifact that incurs zero runtime cost after publication. 

6 Conclusion & Discussion 

In this paper, we present ElasticBack, the conditional single-skill backdoor attack in LLM agents. By semantic-anchored rule injection, ElasticBack plants a dormant rule 

R in the skill document. Considering that a separately gen-erated trigger tends to drift away from the rule, we freeze R

and evolve T to match it with a stealth-constrained genetic search, jointly optimizing effectiveness and stealth. Exten-sive experiments conducted in real-world skills on four types of LLM models demonstrate that ElasticBack significantly outperforms baseline attack methods, achieving both high ASR and low FPR while maintaining normal task perfor-mance. In forthcoming endeavors, we will explore stronger execution-level defenses such as script sandboxing and capa-bility gating against stealthy conditional backdoors in LLM agent skills. Finally, this work is defensive by design, with all experiments sandboxed using benign, sanitized payloads, no harmful attack deployed, and the identified vulnerabilities to be responsibly disclosed.
